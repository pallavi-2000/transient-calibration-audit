\title{Calibration of Production Transient Classifiers: A Benchmark Study}

\author{Pallavi Sati}
\affiliation{University of Bristol, School of Physics, Bristol BS8 1TL, UK}

\begin{abstract}
The Vera C.\ Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time will 
generate $\sim$$10^7$ transient alerts per night against a global 
spectroscopic follow-up capacity of $\sim$$10^5$ observations per year. 
Automated alert brokers address this mismatch by outputting class 
probability vectors, which astronomers use to prioritise follow-up 
observations. These probabilities are implicitly treated as reliable 
confidence estimates, yet no published work has evaluated the 
calibration of production transient classifiers against independent 
spectroscopic ground truth.

We present the first systematic calibration audit of production 
astronomical transient classifiers, evaluating ALeRCE's light curve 
classifier and two Fink classifiers — a Random Forest and SuperNNova 
binary discriminator — on 1,606 spectroscopically confirmed objects 
from the ZTF Bright Transient Survey. We compute Expected Calibration 
Error (ECE) with bootstrap confidence intervals and visualise 
miscalibration via reliability diagrams.

ALeRCE's classifier exhibits systematic underconfidence 
($\mathrm{ECE} = 0.259$; 95\% CI: $[0.237, 0.280]$), with predicted 
probabilities consistently below empirical accuracies across all 
confidence bins. Miscalibration follows an inverse relationship with 
classification accuracy: SNIa and SNIbc, classified with accuracies 
above 0.83, exhibit the worst calibration (ECE $> 0.36$), while SNII, 
classified at 0.495 accuracy, is the best-calibrated class 
(ECE $= 0.103$). This pattern is mechanistically explained by Random 
Forest probability suppression under class imbalance. TDE achieves 
classification accuracy of 0.000 due to a taxonomy gap in ALeRCE's 
output space — a finding with direct operational consequences for 
LSST-era science. Temperature scaling with a single scalar parameter 
($T = 0.359$) reduces ALeRCE's aggregate ECE by 88\% to $0.031$, 
requiring no model retraining.

Fink's classifiers exhibit structural miscalibration 
($\mathrm{ECE} = 0.464$ for RF, $0.304$ for SuperNNova) that 
post-hoc calibration methods cannot adequately address; temperature 
scaling optimisation hits the upper bound for both, indicating 
non-monotonic score distributions incompatible with standard 
calibration techniques.

These results demonstrate that miscalibration is systematic, 
broker-dependent, and consequential for confidence-based 
follow-up prioritisation. Post-hoc calibration can recover 
reliable probabilities for ALeRCE but not for Fink's current 
classifiers. We argue that calibration metrics should be 
reported alongside accuracy for all production astronomical 
classifiers, and that the calibrated ALeRCE outputs provide 
a suitable foundation for deferral-aware classification 
frameworks targeting LSST-era spectroscopic resource allocation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
The Vera C.\ Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time 
\citep[LSST;][]{Ivezic2019} will generate approximately $10^7$ 
transient alerts per night beginning in 2025. Global spectroscopic 
follow-up capacity — the essential confirmation mechanism for 
transient classification — is fixed at roughly $10^5$ observations 
per year \citep{Hambly2022}. This mismatch of four orders of magnitude 
transforms transient astronomy from a data-limited to a 
decision-limited discipline: the central challenge is no longer 
detecting transients but determining which ones warrant the scarce 
resource of spectroscopic confirmation.

Automated alert brokers — ALeRCE \citep{Forster2021}, Fink 
\citep{Moller2021}, ANTARES \citep{Narayan2018}, and Lasair 
\citep{Smith2019} — address this challenge by classifying 
transients from photometric light curves and outputting class 
probability vectors. These probabilities are used by astronomers 
and automated pipelines to prioritise follow-up observations. 
Implicit in this usage is an assumption that the probabilities 
are meaningful confidence estimates — that a classifier outputting 
$P(\mathrm{SNIa}) = 0.9$ is correct approximately 90\% of the time.

This assumption has never been tested. No published work evaluates 
the calibration of production transient classifiers using 
independent spectroscopic ground truth. Reliability diagrams and 
Expected Calibration Error \citep[ECE;][]{Naeini2015} — standard 
diagnostic tools in machine learning — are absent from the 
astronomical broker literature. If production classifiers are 
systematically miscalibrated, the confidence-based prioritisation 
decisions made by astronomers and automated systems are built on 
unreliable foundations.

In this paper we conduct the first systematic calibration audit 
of production transient classifiers, evaluating ALeRCE's light 
curve classifier and two Fink classifiers on 1,606 
spectroscopically confirmed objects from the ZTF Bright Transient 
Survey \citep{Fremling2020, Perley2020}. Our principal findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item ALeRCE's classifier exhibits systematic underconfidence 
($\mathrm{ECE} = 0.259$), not the overconfidence typical of 
deep learning models. Predicted probabilities are consistently 
lower than empirical accuracies across all confidence bins.

\item The miscalibration follows an inverse relationship with 
accuracy: the best-classified types (SNIa, SNIbc; accuracy $>0.83$) 
are the most severely underconfident (ECE $>0.36$), while the 
worst-classified type (SNII; accuracy $0.495$) is the 
best-calibrated (ECE $0.103$).

\item Temperature scaling with a single scalar parameter 
($T = 0.359$) reduces ALeRCE's ECE by 88\%, to $0.031$ — 
below the threshold for practically significant miscalibration, 
with no model retraining required.

\item TDE, a high-priority LSST science target, achieves 
classification accuracy of $0.000$ on ALeRCE because TDE 
is absent from ALeRCE's output taxonomy.

\item Fink's classifiers exhibit structural miscalibration 
($\mathrm{ECE} = 0.464$ for RF, $0.304$ for SuperNNova) 
that post-hoc calibration methods cannot adequately address.
\end{enumerate}

These findings have immediate implications for spectroscopic 
follow-up prioritisation and motivate the deferral-aware 
classification framework we develop in subsequent work.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
We have presented the first systematic calibration audit of production 
astronomical transient classifiers, evaluating ALeRCE's light curve 
classifier and two Fink classifiers against 1,606 spectroscopically 
confirmed ZTF transients from the Bright Transient Survey. Our results 
demonstrate that miscalibration is systematic, broker-dependent, and 
consequential for confidence-based follow-up prioritisation.

ALeRCE's classifier exhibits systematic underconfidence driven by 
Random Forest probability suppression under class imbalance 
($\mathrm{ECE} = 0.259$). This miscalibration is correctable: 
temperature scaling with $T = 0.359$ reduces ECE by 88\% to $0.031$, 
requiring no model retraining. The correction is not uniform across 
classes — SNII, the only well-calibrated class before scaling, 
worsens under a global temperature parameter — motivating class-aware 
calibration schemes for future work. TDE classification fails entirely 
due to a taxonomy gap in ALeRCE's output space, an operational problem 
requiring resolution before LSST first light. Fink's classifiers exhibit 
structural miscalibration that post-hoc methods cannot adequately address, 
with temperature scaling optimisation hitting the upper bound for both 
classifiers.

These findings establish three priorities for LSST-era broker 
development. First, calibration metrics — ECE, reliability diagrams, 
and per-class confidence intervals — should be reported alongside 
accuracy for all production classifiers as standard practice. Second, 
post-hoc calibration layers should be integrated into broker pipelines; 
the negligible computational cost of temperature scaling is justified 
by the substantial improvement in probability reliability. Third, 
taxonomy completeness must be verified against LSST science priorities 
before deployment. The calibrated ALeRCE outputs demonstrated here 
provide a suitable empirical foundation for the deferral-aware 
classification framework we develop in subsequent work, where reliable 
confidence estimates are prerequisite to learning when to involve 
human astronomers in follow-up decisions.
